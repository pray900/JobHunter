# CV and cover letter craft

This is know-how for the writing itself. The facts, the exact-copy rules, the limits and the output format come from your system prompt. Here you will find how to make good writing within those rules.

## Why the wording matters

Applicant tracking systems (ATS) read the text of a document first and often fail silently on layout. Ranking tends to compare the responsibilities in the ad with the experience text in the CV. Then a recruiter skims for a few seconds. So the CV has to parse cleanly, echo the ad's duties honestly, and show a result a skimming reader can catch.

## Bullets: responsibility plus outcome

Write each bullet as what the person was responsible for, then what came of it, using the ad's own duty wording and terminology where it is true.

If the ad asks you to "maintain and improve reporting dashboards for operations managers", a matching bullet reads:

- Maintained and improved the weekly operations dashboards used by four site managers, cutting report preparation from two hours to thirty minutes.

Not:

- Worked on dashboards and did various reporting tasks.

Habits that help:

- Lead with the outcome or the scale when there is one, and with the responsibility when there is not.
- Use the ad's noun phrases for the work ("stakeholder reporting", "rostering", "month-end close") when the base facts support them. Do not swap them for fancier synonyms. A system matching words and a person scanning for them both want the ad's term.
- One idea per bullet. Two lines at most.
- Vary the rhythm. Some bullets start with a verb, some with the outcome, some with the scope ("Across three regional offices, ..."). A column of "Verb + X, resulting in Y%" reads as machine-made.
- Start strong bullets with plain verbs: built, ran, reduced, trained, scheduled, wrote, fixed. Avoid verbs that claim more than the facts do.
- Cut padding: "responsible for", "helped with", "various", "etc.", "involved in".
- Put the most relevant bullet first in each role. A short role with one or two good bullets beats a padded one.

## Keywords

Keywords come from the ad's duties, tools, qualifications, soft skills and years-of-experience terms.

- Place a keyword where it belongs in a true sentence: the headline, the summary, the skills group, or a bullet that actually did that work. One honest use in context counts for more than five in a list.
- Soft skills are keywords too ("stakeholder management", "mentoring", "communication"). Show them through a bullet ("Mentored two new starters through their first quarter") instead of writing the label alone. Use the label where it fits naturally.
- Years terms ("3+ years") only appear when the facts support them, in the same words the facts use.
- Spell a term the way the ad does. If the ad writes the full name and the acronym ("Customer Relationship Management (CRM)"), write the full name and acronym once, then use either. If the ad uses only the acronym, use the acronym.
- Never stuff. No keyword lists in prose, no repeating a term to raise a count, no hidden or white text. A keyword that the facts cannot support stays out of the CV.

## Result and scale figures

A number makes a recruiter stop scanning. Where the facts state a figure, use it exactly. Where a bullet has no figure and one would help, a modest estimate is allowed for experience and project bullets, if your system prompt allows it.

Make an added figure:

- Realistic for the role, the seniority and the employer's size. A part-time retail assistant does not cut costs by 40 per cent. A person at a ten-person agency does not manage a 200-person programme.
- Modest. "About a quarter fewer", "around 30 a week", "under a day" are believable. Round, huge or very precise numbers are not.
- Consistent with the bullet's own facts: no larger team, longer time or wider scope than the facts imply.
- Kept to bullets. Not the headline, the summary, education or dates.
- Listed as a stretch, so the person can check it before sending: say it is an estimate that does not come from the facts.

A bullet with a good scale but no outcome can still be strong: "Triaged 25 to 30 support tickets a day across email and chat" shows volume without claiming a result.

## ATS-safe wording

- Use standard section headings: Summary, Skills, Experience, Projects, Education. Do not invent clever ones ("My journey", "Toolbox").
- Plain text only for content. No icons, emoji, arrows, ratings (stars, dots, bars) or decorative bullets. A skill level belongs in words, if at all.
- No tables, columns or text boxes. Put one thing per line.
- Write dates in one consistent format.
- Write technology names the way the industry does (JavaScript, PostgreSQL, Power BI).
- Keep the summary to the point: who the person is and what they offer this employer, in up to three short lines. Do not hand the ad's wording back to the employer.

## Cover letter

The letter is short, specific and human. It is not the CV in paragraphs.

1. Open on the employer's need. Say what the role needs, from the ad, and why that fits the person. For example: "Your team is looking for someone to keep the month-end close on time while the entity count grows. That is the work I have been doing for the last two years." Do not open with "I am writing to apply" or a line about being excited.
2. One proof paragraph. Pick the one or two facts that matter most for this job and tell them as a small story: the situation, what the person did, what came of it. Use a figure only from the facts, the ad or the CV just written.
3. If the facts and the ad support it, a short paragraph on fit with the employer. Only say what the ad or notes say about the company. Do not guess at its products, values or news.
4. A plain close. Say the person would welcome a conversation, and thank the reader for their time. No pressure, no flattery, no summary of what was just said.

Keep each paragraph to three or four sentences. Use contractions sparingly and the person's own tone from the sample letter. Address a named contact only if the ad or notes name one.

## Before you finish

Read it once as a recruiter with a few seconds, and once as a text parser. Does the top third say why this person suits the role? Does every bullet say what was done and, where honest, what came of it? Do the ad's own terms appear where they are true? Does anything claim more than the facts? If so, cut it.

Your system prompt's rules win over anything here.
